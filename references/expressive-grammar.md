# FigJam expressive grammar

Read this reference for bold, data-rich, dashboard, portfolio, chart, visual-metaphor, or presentation-style FigJam work. Expression can make the argument memorable and the workspace inviting. Playful atmosphere and delight are worthwhile even when they do not encode information; protect reading clarity and room to work.

## Choose one dominant move

Write the section's argument in one sentence, then choose the visual move that makes it easiest to see:

- Scale for magnitude, priority, or a single decisive number.
- Position for sequence, hierarchy, or convergence.
- Shape for role, state, category, or a literal metaphor.
- Color for supplied status, category, or attention semantics.
- A chart for a real quantitative relationship.
- Connectors for dependencies or movement.

One hero visual should own the first glance. Supporting type, marks, and containers should reinforce it at lower volume. If several shapes, colors, charts, and display-type treatments all compete for first glance, the section has not chosen its argument.

Bold does not mean saturated everywhere. A large near-black numeral, an oversized path, or a strongly asymmetric composition can carry more visual weight than a rainbow grid. Use the smallest number of strong moves that makes the board feel authored.

## Give every visual variable one job

Do not ask the same variable to mean two things in one board. If color encodes status, do not also use unrelated colors merely to create variety. If circle size encodes magnitude, do not enlarge one circle just because it needs emphasis.

Use a simple weight budget:

- One primary focal object per section.
- Two or three supporting objects.
- A clear first-glance focus; multiple vibrant regions are allowed when the hierarchy remains legible.
- One dominant data encoding per metric group.

Large shapes and saturated fields consume more of the budget than labels, quiet strokes, or neutral cards. Spend visual weight where the argument is strongest.

## Avoid motif autopilot

A left bar, accent stripe, short rule, tinted card, or badge is not a default sign for "important." Repeating one of these across unrelated roles makes the board feel templated and gives the mark no stable meaning.

Use bars or stripes only when they express a real boundary, quotation, selected path, status, or category. State that job in the build plan and audit. If the role is merely emphasis, first try scale, weight, spacing, position, or a content-specific shape.

Apply the same test to every repeated motif: repetition is useful only when the role repeats. Deliberately vary the dominant form across different section jobs. A metric can be numeral-led, a dependency model path-led, and a comparison table cell-led without losing coherence because type, palette, spacing, and alignment still bind the board together.

## Make charts honest and legible

Use a chart only when the supplied data contains a real relationship to encode. Read [data-viz-grammar.md](data-viz-grammar.md) and build its analytical substrate before adding an expressive layer. Do not infer time series, denominators, intervals, or category shares that were not provided.

- Raw count or single value: large numeral with a concise label.
- Bounded proportion with a known denominator: progress bar, ring, or part-to-whole mark.
- Trend with ordered observations: sparkline or line chart, with direction and endpoints readable.
- Category comparison: aligned bars or dots on a common baseline.
- Distribution: histogram or dot plot only when bins or observations exist.
- Network or dependency: nodes and connectors, not a statistical chart.

Size the chart like the point when it is the evidence. Label units, baseline, endpoints, and source when needed to prevent misreading. Keep ornamental grid lines, fake precision, gradients, and duplicated legends out. At overview zoom, the relationship should remain visible even if fine labels require reading zoom.

When a current value and a trend support the same claim, compose them as one visual system rather than equal peer panels. Let the value lead and make the trend visibly explain it.

Limit answer echo. The headline and chart may reinforce the same claim; an additional hero number, side panel, or footer must answer a different question or remove real inference. Delete repeated readouts that add visual weight without adding understanding.

For dependencies, expressive weight must not weaken traceability. Use heavy or saturated paths only after each relationship can be traced independently. Shared arrival points at a node are acceptable when direction remains clear; ambiguous shared trunks are not. Prefer resize-safe edge attachments over bespoke ports, and keep playful marks out of connector corridors.

## Use color and shape semantically

Information-bearing color should answer a question such as status, category, path, or attention. Use slate-blue for structural facts and orange for friction or negative movement. Reserve alarm treatments for genuinely critical or action-blocking states. Ornamental color is welcome when it cannot be mistaken for evidence or status and does not compete with the primary takeaway.

Shape should shorten explanation. Circles can carry actors or bounded progress, rectangles can carry evidence or steps, diamonds can carry decisions, and a literal silhouette can fulfill a metaphorical title. The metaphor must remain recognizable at overview zoom and must not distort the underlying information. In a comparison, it should carry at least one supplied distinction among the alternatives and remain primary or co-primary rather than becoming an appended illustration.

Custom vectors, text paths, and boolean shapes can create legible metaphors, handmade warmth, or playful atmosphere. Loose clusters, gentle asymmetry, and small illustrations are options, not a required checklist. Use quiet warmth for incidents. Do not fabricate votes, approvals, signatures, or participant reactions. Only call an object a native stamp or sticker when that primitive was actually used; editable vector art is a valid alternative, not proof of native sticker support.

## Compose for overview and reading zoom

At overview zoom, the reader should identify the primary claim or object within two seconds, then the supporting relationship. Use asymmetry and negative space to isolate the focal object, but balance the section silhouette so it does not feel abandoned on one side.

At reading zoom, verify that headings, subtitles, labels, axes, legends, and sources maintain their role hierarchy. Measure all text after final width, weight, and line height; reflow every downstream object. No expressive move earns permission to overlap, clip, obscure labels, or reduce supporting type below the normalized overview floor.

## Run the meaning audit

For every large shape, color field, chart, connector system, or repeated mark, record:

1. Does this encode information, establish atmosphere, or invite participation?
2. What should the reader notice or feel because of it?
3. Does it obstruct reading, tracing, or working, or repeat a flourish mechanically?

Reduce or move elements that obstruct use; keep delightful touches that coexist with clarity. Then check the whole board:

- The first-glance object matches the intended argument.
- Visual weight matches argument weight.
- Color and shape have stable meanings.
- Chart grammar matches the supplied data.
- No generic motif repeats across unrelated roles.
- Overview and reading zoom remain legible and collision-free.
- The result feels deliberately made, expressive, and appropriate to its task without sacrificing usability.
